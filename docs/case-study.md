# Case study: designing a custom cake without a misunderstanding

## The problem

Custom cakes are usually ordered by message or phone. The customer describes a cake in words
and sends a few reference photos; the bakery interprets them. Information is lost at every step:
the flavour of each tier, the real size of "a cake for thirty people", what "gold details" means.
Mistakes are discovered at pickup, when nothing can be changed.

The idea started with a personal experience as a customer: flavours misunderstood in an order
placed over WhatsApp. Market research then showed it was not an isolated case. Reading public
Google Reviews of patisseries and bakeries, complaints about custom orders kept returning to the
same themes: a different flavour from the one requested, and a finished cake that did not match
what the customer had asked for.

This is qualitative research on public reviews. It is not a statistical study, and it does not
describe any Norvy client.

## How structured configuration reduces ambiguity

- **Every choice is a value, not a sentence.** Shape, diameter, height, sponge, cream, covering
  and colour are picked from the bakery's own options, which leaves far less to interpret.
- **Each tier is described on its own.** Flavour and filling are set tier by tier, so "chocolate
  at the bottom, pistachio on top" is recorded tier by tier rather than in a message.
- **The customer previews the intended design.** The 3D view shows the cake, inside and out,
  before the order; the review step lists every tier and choice.
- **Sizes are tied to guests.** The servings helper relates the number of guests to sizes and
  tiers, instead of a guess.
- **The bakery receives the same design.** The order carries the exact design the customer
  approved, with a price recomputed on the server.

## Who it is for

- **Businesses that sell custom cakes**: patisseries, bakeries, franchises and online cake shops
  that want orders to arrive complete and unambiguous.
- **Customers**, most of them not cake professionals, many ordering in Arabic on a phone. They
  need to preview the cake, not imagine it, and to understand sizes in terms of guests.
- **Bakery owners and staff**, who need an exact, printable description of what to make, a price
  they can trust, and their orders, capacity and costs in one place.
- **Shops with a counter**, where customers design on a touch screen in store.

## What was built

A browser-based 3D configurator in which the customer composes the cake step by step:
structure (tiers, sizes, shapes, separators, dummy tiers), flavours and fillings, covering,
finishes, decorations and lettering, then review and order. The bakery side receives the design
with a server-computed price and manages it through an order dashboard, also available as a
desktop application. A second configurator for gift boxes of small sweets runs on the same
platform.

## Decisions that shaped the product

**Show a realistic preview.** Customers judge a cake by its surface: soft fondant edges, the texture
of buttercream, the shine of a glaze. That's why the cake tiers, the main coverings and pastry
decorations are modelled by hand in Blender, and why realistic textures took the most work.
Parts that have to follow every shape and choice (display tiers, drips, ruffles, borders,
lettering) are generated by the product while the customer designs.

**Full control, without presets imposed.** The differentiator is that the customer controls the
structure: separators, tilt, the filling of each tier, dummy tiers. These controls stay visible.
Clarity, not a reduced feature set, is what makes the tool usable by non-professionals.

**Arabic first.** Right-to-left layout, Arabic terminology for pastry techniques, Gulf currency
formats and an in-store kiosk mode are part of the core, not a translation layer.

**Trust in the price.** The price a customer sees is recomputed on the server from the design
before anything is charged.

**A platform, not a page.** The same server, catalogue, order flow and dashboard serve both the
cake and gift-box configurators, and a shop's own appearance is applied through themes.

## What was hard

- Keeping a detailed 3D scene smooth on mid-range phones and on Windows laptops whose browsers
  compile shaders slowly (see [ENGINEERING.md](../ENGINEERING.md)).
- Making decorations and finishes fit four shapes and every tier height.
- Building an interface that a first-time customer understands on a phone, in Arabic, without
  hiding the controls that make the product worth using.
